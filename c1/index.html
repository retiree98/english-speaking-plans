The idea

At C1 the work is precision, not fluency

At C1 you already speak fluently and spontaneously. People understand you, and you rarely stop to search for words. The next stage, C2, is about precision (the exact word), nuance (hinting, softening, emphasising), natural phrasing (idioms and collocations a native speaker would choose), register (formal or friendly, to fit the person), handling the unexpected, and holding a long, well-organised talk. It also means fixing the few mistakes you repeat. That work comes from careful practice with feedback, so both plans spend most of the time on your voice: shadowing native speakers, recording yourself, and talking with people and AI that push back.

The General plan builds the English of an educated, confident speaker: opinions with nuance, debate, storytelling, phone calls and complaints, and explaining complex ideas simply.

The Quran plan uses the material you already love. You explain tajweed exactly, to adults and to children; talk about the meaning of short surahs (tafsir) in English; give short talks to parents; handle sensitive conversations politely; and lead a class fully in English. About a third of the time (conversations, AI chats) stays on everyday English, so daily life improves too.

You also have a useful ear. Arabic has long and short vowels and doubled letters, so you are used to hearing small differences in sound and length. At C1 this skill moves from single sounds to the melody of whole sentences: where the voice pauses, rises, falls and stresses a word.

You have a rare advantage. Tajweed trained your ear and mouth to notice tiny differences in sound and length. At C1 this skill moves from single sounds to the melody of whole sentences: where the voice pauses, rises, falls and stresses a word.

Why this works

  • A balanced diet of English. Paul Nation's “four strands” model gives roughly equal time to listening, speaking, studying language and fluency practice. At C1 the “studying” strand is mostly collocations, idioms and register, learned from real speech.
  • Native-speed listening. At C1, most new words and phrases come from long, natural listening (podcasts, lectures, talks), so the sources are made for native listeners, not learners.
  • Native-speed listening. At C1, most new words and phrases come from long, natural listening (podcasts, lectures, khutbahs), so the sources are made for native listeners, not learners.
  • Shadowing (speaking along with a recording) improves listening and the rhythm, stress and intonation of speech, which is the main sound work at C1.
  • Repeating the same task makes speech smoother and more accurate each time. That is why you record, listen and record again, and why the 5/4/3 drill repeats one talk three times.
  • Spaced flashcards are one of the strongest findings in learning research: reviewing just before you forget makes phrases stick. Collect whole phrases, not single words.
  • Clear and natural. Research on being understood puts consonants, vowel length and word stress first. When those are solid, as they are at C1, sentence stress and intonation are what make speech easy and pleasant to follow.

Realistic expectations. Cambridge estimates about 200 hours of guided learning to move up one level, and progress feels slower at the higher levels. These 12 weeks give about 60 focused hours. They will not take you to C2. They will make your English more precise, more natural and more confident, and they will reduce your most repeated mistakes. Reaching C2 usually takes a year or more of steady work, so after week 12 you repeat the cycle with new material.

Week 0 · one evening

Set up and record your starting point

Every day · about 50 minutes, plus native-speed listening

Daily routine

Blocks 1 and 3 are the same every day. Block 2 changes by day (see the weekly rhythm). The listening fits into time you already have.

15 min

Listen and shadow

Choose 1–2 minutes of a native-speed recording: Luke's English Podcast, a TED talk, or a BBC podcast. Listen once, read the transcript once, then speak with the speaker, copying the stress, melody and pauses, not only the words. Repeat 3–4 times, then mark one phrase you want to steal.

Choose 1–2 minutes of a native-speed talk: a lecture on the Yaqeen Institute site, an English khutbah, or Mufti Menk's Stories of the Prophets. Listen once, read the captions once, then speak with the speaker, copying the stress, melody and pauses, not only the words. Repeat 3–4 times, then mark one phrase you want to steal.

25 min

Today's speaking focus

The day's task from the weekly rhythm. Always out loud, and usually recorded. When you listen back, check three things: the exact words, the sound of the sentence, and your top-mistakes list.

10 min

Phrases and review

Add 5 useful phrases from today to your notebook and Anki: collocations, idioms, or a more natural way to say something you said. Add the correct version of any mistake you made. Review old cards. Say each phrase aloud in a full sentence about your own life.

Add 5 phrases from today to Anki: collocations, teaching phrases, or a more natural way to say something you said. Add the correct version of any mistake you made. Then read the English meaning and tafsir of a few verses from a short surah you teach, on Quran.com (open a verse's tafsir and choose Ibn Kathir (Abridged), which is in English), and say the main idea aloud in 2–3 sentences.

20–30 min

Native-speed listening, anytime

While cooking, walking or travelling, listen to English made for native listeners: a Luke's English Podcast episode, In Our Time on BBC Radio 4, or a TED talk. It is fine to understand 90%. No notes, no pressure. This is where idioms and natural phrasing quietly come from.

The two rules that keep you going. On a busy or tired day, do the 10-minute minimum: one shadowing round and one recording. It still counts. And never miss two days in a row: one missed day is normal; two in a row is how plans stop.

All day, free: think in English, and choose the exact word. When you describe your day in your head, ask “is there a more natural way to say that?” (“I'm going to call the clinic” → “I need to get through to the clinic and sort out an appointment.”) When a word is missing, note it and look it up later.

All day, free: think in English, and choose the exact word. When you prepare tomorrow's lesson in your head, practise explaining it two ways: simply for a child, and precisely for an adult. When a word is missing, note it and look it up later.

Phase 1 · Weeks 1–4

Natural sound, natural phrases

Goal: notice how native speakers stress and link words, start using collocations and idioms on purpose, and talk for 2 minutes with clear sentence stress and fewer repeated errors.

Phase 2 · Weeks 5–8

Nuance and the unexpected

Goal: hold your own in debate, soften and sharpen your opinions, keep calm when a conversation goes off-plan, tell a story with detail, and talk for 3 minutes in a clear structure.

Goal: explain the finer points of tajweed to adults, give the meaning of short surahs in English, and speak for 3 minutes in a clear structure, to parents or to adult students.

Phase 3 · Weeks 9–12

Long talks and confident control

Goal: handle phone calls, complaints and abstract topics in the right register, and give a 10-minute talk from short notes that is well organised and easy to follow.

Goal: handle sensitive parent conversations politely, lead a class fully in English, and teach a 20-minute lesson (or play a full parent-meeting role-play) from short notes.

Prosody for Arabic speakers · both plans

From sounds to the melody of speech

At C1 your individual sounds are mostly clear. What still marks speech as non-native, and what makes long talks tiring to follow, is usually the music of the sentence: which words are stressed, where the voice rises and falls, and how words join. Your Arabic helps here, because Arabic also uses length, pauses and the joining of words.

Precision and nuance

Phrases for precise, natural speech

These phrases are for the situations in the 12-week map. Say each one aloud, then change it to fit your own opinions and your own life. Learn them in chunks, not word by word.

Softening and hedging

  • I'd say it's fair to argue that…
  • To some extent that's true, but…
  • It seems to me that…
  • I tend to think… / I'm inclined to think…
  • That may well be the case, although…
  • With respect, I see it a little differently.

Agreeing, partly agreeing, pushing back

  • I couldn't agree more.
  • I see your point, but I'd push back on one thing.
  • That's true up to a point, but it overlooks…
  • Let me play devil's advocate for a moment.
  • Fair enough. I hadn't thought of it that way.
  • I'm not convinced. What makes you say that?

Buying time and repairing

  • That's a good question. Let me think for a second.
  • What I mean is… / Let me put it another way.
  • Sorry, I didn't express that well. What I meant was…
  • If I understand you correctly, you're saying…
  • Could you elaborate on that?
  • Going back to what you said earlier…

Formal or friendly, idioms and collocations

  • Formal: I would be grateful if you could… / Friendly: Could you do me a favour?
  • Formal: I regret to inform you… / Friendly: I'm afraid I've got bad news.
  • make a decision, take a risk, reach an agreement, raise a concern
  • a heavy workload, a tight deadline, a steep learning curve
  • to get the ball rolling · to be on the same page · to see eye to eye
  • to bite the bullet · a blessing in disguise · the bottom line is…

In class (adults and children)

  • Today we'll focus on one idea: where the sound comes from.
  • Listen carefully to the difference between these two letters.
  • Try it again, and this time raise the back of your tongue.
  • You've got the length right. Now work on the heaviness.
  • I'm going to model it first, and then you try.
  • Good effort. The difference is small, so let's slow down.
  • Can you explain the rule back to me in your own words?
  • Let's recap what we covered today.

With parents (sensitive moments)

  • I'd like to share some thoughts on how she's getting on.
  • He's making steady progress, though the long sounds still need work.
  • Every child learns at their own pace, so I'd avoid comparing him with others.
  • I understand it's been a busy period. Could we find a routine that works for you?
  • I'm a little concerned about the missed lessons, because the rules build on each other.
  • May I suggest a few ways you could support her at home?
  • I appreciate you raising that. Let me look into it and get back to you.
  • Please don't worry. This is common at this stage.

Giving a talk or a tafsir

  • I'd like to talk about three things: the context, the meaning and the lesson.
  • This surah was revealed at a time when…
  • The word used here suggests…
  • Scholars explain that…
  • What does this teach us about patience / gratitude / trust?
  • To sum up, the main point is…

Speaking practice · both plans

Three ways to get speaking time

1 · Record yourself

The most important habit. Speak for 2–3 minutes, listen back, note 3 things to improve (one about content, one about wording, one about sound), then record again. Any mistake you hear more than once goes on your top-mistakes list. Compare recordings month by month, and ask a good speaker to listen to one recording every two weeks.

2 · Talk with people, twice a week

Real conversation is where nuance grows: 30–40 minutes on Wednesday and 20 minutes on Monday. Language exchange on Tandem or HelloTalk (free tiers): you help them with Arabic, they help you with English. Ask native or very fluent partners to point out anything that sounds unnatural, during or after the talk. Or use a sister, friend or colleague who speaks English well.

3 · Talk with AI

Free voice chat in the ChatGPT or Gemini app, as extra practice or when no one is free. Ask it to speak at natural speed and to push back, so you practise defending your ideas. Free tiers have daily limits, so 15–20 minutes is the right size. Use the prompt below.
